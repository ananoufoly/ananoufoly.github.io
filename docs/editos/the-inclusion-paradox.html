The inclusion paradox

On xenophobia, economic integration, and what South Africa reveals about the continent

EN 20 May 2026
South Africaxenophobiafinancial integrationAfCFTA

South Africa is, by the metrics that appear in the standard indices, one of the most financially developed economies on the African continent. It has a sophisticated banking sector, a deep capital market, a central bank with a credible inflation-targeting framework, and a regulatory architecture that compares favourably with many middle-income countries elsewhere in the world. It is also a country where waves of violence against migrants from elsewhere in Africa recur with troubling regularity.

These two facts are not incidental to each other. They sit at the heart of what might be called the inclusion paradox: the observation that formal financial inclusion — access to accounts, credit, insurance, payments — does not automatically translate into economic inclusion in the broader sense. A country can score well on the GSMA Mobile Money adoption index and score badly on the human development of its most marginal residents. These are different dimensions of the same underlying question.

The African Continental Free Trade Area imagines a different kind of continent — one where goods, services, capital, and eventually people move more freely across borders that were, after all, drawn by others. The ambition is real. So is the gap between the ambition and the current political economy of most member states, including South Africa.
