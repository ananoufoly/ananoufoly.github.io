Togo has announced that it will ask the United Nations General Assembly in September to replace the Mercator projection with a map that more accurately represents the true size of the world's continents. It is a symbolic gesture — the kind that tends to attract knowing smiles in the corridors of international organisations — but it points to something real: the maps we use to see the world shape the arguments we can make within it.

The Mercator projection, designed in 1569 for maritime navigation, inflates landmasses as they approach the poles. Greenland appears roughly the size of Africa. In reality, Africa is fourteen times larger. This is not a minor distortion. It is the kind of distortion that, reproduced on classroom walls and in newspaper graphics for generations, quietly shapes intuitions about who is big and who is small, who is central and who is peripheral.

Togo's initiative sits at the intersection of the symbolic and the substantive. The country is also, in a different register, engaged in a serious debate about financial inclusion — about who has access to basic financial services, on what terms, and at what cost. The two conversations are not as far apart as they might seem. Both are about the gap between the map and the territory: the gap between how things are represented and how they actually are.
